Social feed and timelines

How do I build a home timeline that shows posts from everyone I follow?

Applications like this: social networks, activity feeds, notifications. This page is written the way I write a database design doc: the workload first, then where each kind of data goes and why, then how it grows, what breaks, and what I rejected.

The short answerPosts and the follow graph in a relational database. Timelines precomputed per user in Redis or a wide-column store (fan-out on write), except for accounts with huge followings, whose posts are merged in at read time.

1 · The workload

What the application asks of the database

DimensionThis application
WritesPosts are moderate. Fan-out multiplies each post by the number of followers.
ReadsVery high. Opening the app reads a timeline.
Read to writeHigh, often more than 100 to 1.
ConsistencyLoose. A post can appear a few seconds late.
LatencyThe first screen of the timeline in well under a second.
GrowthFast, driven by user count times follows.
2 · What goes where

Each kind of data, its store, and why

Solid arrows: the application writes there. Dashed arrows: a copy fed from the source of truth (change data capture, an outbox, or a cache fill), which can be rebuilt from it.

DataWhere it goesWhy
Users, posts, followsPostgreSQL or MySQLThe record of who posted what and who follows whom.
Precomputed timelinesRedis lists or sorted sets, or Cassandra and ScyllaDB at large scaleA timeline is a list of post IDs per user, read as one key. Writes are many and small, which suits memory or an LSM store.

3 · Data model

The core tables and the queries they serve

-- record of truth
CREATE TABLE follows (follower_id bigint, followee_id bigint,
                      PRIMARY KEY (follower_id, followee_id));
CREATE INDEX ON follows (followee_id);

# timeline per user, newest first, capped at 800 entries
ZADD timeline:42 1763452800 post:9001
ZREMRANGEBYRANK timeline:42 0 -801
ZREVRANGE timeline:42 0 49
  • Fan-out on write: when someone posts, a worker pushes the post ID into each follower's timeline.
  • For accounts with millions of followers, skip fan-out and merge their recent posts in at read time. A hybrid is the usual answer.
  • Timelines are a cache. They can be rebuilt from posts and follows.
4 · How it grows

Start simple, change one thing at a time

  1. Pull modelAt small scale, query the posts of everyone I follow, newest first, with a good index. No precomputation.
  2. Reads get expensivePrecompute timelines in Redis with fan-out on write.
  3. Memory gets expensiveMove timelines to a wide-column store partitioned by user_id, with only the hot ones in Redis.
5 · What breaks first

The failures to plan for

What happensWhat you seeWhat I do
A celebrity postsThe fan-out queue backs up for minutesDo not fan out for accounts above a follower threshold. Merge at read time.
Pull queries at scaleA big IN list of followees sorted by timePrecompute.
Timelines that never expireMemory grows with inactive usersCap each timeline, and drop timelines of users who have not opened the app in weeks.
6 · Alternatives

What I considered and did not pick

  • A graph database. Good for deep graph questions (friends of friends of friends). A timeline is a one-hop question that relational tables and caches answer well.
  • Everything in one relational database at large scale. The fan-out writes alone would dominate the primary.

Cost. Memory for timelines is the big line. Cap timeline length and drop inactive users' timelines.
